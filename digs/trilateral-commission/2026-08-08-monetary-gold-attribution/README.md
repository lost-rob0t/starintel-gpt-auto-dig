# Trilateral Commission monetary-gold attribution pass — 2026-08-08

## Scope

This pass applies the policy-attribution tiers to Triangle Paper 1, `Towards a Renovated World Monetary System` (1973), and the later U.S./IMF gold-sale and developing-country Trust Fund policy.

The purpose is to distinguish three separate facts:

1. the Trilateral report really recommended coordinated official gold sales and development use of gains;
2. later U.S./IMF policy materially resembled that recommendation; and
3. the documentary record currently reviewed does **not** establish that the Trilateral Commission caused or uniquely originated the government/IMF policy.

## Finding 1 — the 1973 Trilateral recommendation is authentic

The IMF's own selected bibliography identifies `Towards a Renovated World Monetary System`, Triangle Paper No. 1, as a 1973 Trilateral Monetary Task Force report by Motoo Kaji, Richard N. Cooper, and Claudio Segré.

A digitized copy of the 44-page report reproduces its summary and gold-policy section. The report recommended, as an urgent transitional measure, cooperative and coordinated sales of official gold into private markets and proposed transferring the resulting capital gains to international financial institutions for development assistance.

Because the readable full text currently comes from a third-party digitization rather than a Trilateral/archival scan, the exact wording should carry lower source confidence than the IMF bibliographic verification. The substance is nonetheless clear enough to define the proposition being tested.

## Finding 2 — closely similar U.S. gold-policy proposals appear almost immediately, but without a Commission citation

A Federal Reserve memorandum dated **November 1, 1973** — only days after the Trilateral task-force report was presented to the Commission Executive Committee on October 22–23 — records U.S. objectives including allowing central-bank market sales of gold and reducing gold's international monetary role.

A Federal Reserve paper dated **March 15, 1974** goes further and proposes cooperative arrangements among major governments for official gold sales into the private market, preferably using the IMF as marketing agent.

A June 1974 U.S. policy paper says the IMF should be permitted gradually to sell gold to obtain additional resources to assist members.

These records establish a strong policy parallel and chronological proximity. They do **not** cite the Trilateral paper. With the evidence currently available, they should be classified as `parallel_policy_track / attribution_unresolved`, not as Commission implementation.

## Finding 3 — the development-assistance mechanism was independently articulated inside the Ford administration

The government record provides multiple non-Trilateral steps before Carter took office:

- **November 14, 1974:** Henry Kissinger publicly proposed a special IMF trust fund for the poorest oil-affected developing countries and said IMF gold-sale profits could contribute to it.
- **January 1975:** Treasury Secretary William Simon reported the U.S. proposal for a trust fund financed partly by profits from IMF gold sales.
- **May 19, 1975:** a Treasury paper proposed an IMF-administered trust fund using funds acquired through gradual market resale of gold transferred from the IMF at the official price.
- **August 31, 1975:** the IMF Interim Committee reached agreement on sale of one-sixth of IMF gold, 25 million ounces, for the benefit of developing countries.
- **May 5, 1976:** the IMF Executive Board established the Trust Fund.
- **June 2, 1976:** the first IMF gold auction was held.

IMF records say Trust Fund resources were principally the profits from those gold sales and were used for concessional balance-of-payments assistance to low-income members.

## Finding 4 — the common claim that Cooper entered the Carter administration and then implemented his Trilateral gold recommendation is contradicted by chronology

The State Department's official principal-officer history says Richard N. Cooper was appointed Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs on **April 7, 1977** and entered duty on **April 8, 1977**.

By then:

- the IMF gold-sale agreement had been reached for roughly 19 months;
- the Trust Fund had existed for 11 months; and
- IMF gold auctions had been operating for about 10 months.

Therefore Cooper's later Carter-administration appointment cannot explain the origin or initial implementation of the IMF gold-sale/Trust Fund mechanism.

This does **not** disprove all possible intellectual influence of the 1973 report. It disproves the narrower personnel-based implementation story.

## Attribution classification

### Supported

- `Trilateral paper recommended`: coordinated official gold sales into private markets + use of gains for development assistance.
- `later U.S./IMF policy parallel`: coordinated/official market gold sales, diminished monetary role for gold, and use of IMF gold-sale profits for developing-country assistance.
- `Trilateral recommendation predates final IMF agreement`: yes.

### Not established

- direct government citation of Triangle Paper 1 in the reviewed Federal Reserve, Treasury, Ford White House, or IMF decision record;
- Trilateral Commission authorship of the later U.S./IMF mechanism;
- Richard Cooper implementing the mechanism after joining Carter's government.

### Best current label

`policy_parallel_with_chronological_precedence; causal_attribution_unresolved; personnel_implementation_claim_contradicted`

## Why this matters for StarIntel

This case is a good negative-control test for graph reasoning. A naive graph would see:

`Cooper → authored Trilateral report → later Carter official → IMF gold policy similar to report`

and infer implementation.

The dates break that inference. The mechanism was agreed and operating before Cooper joined the Carter administration.

The graph therefore needs chronology-aware policy attribution, not merely path existence.
