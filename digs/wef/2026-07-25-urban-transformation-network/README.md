# WEF influence in Columbus, Ohio

**Research date:** 2026-07-25  
**Dataset:** `starintel-wef-columbus-urban-transformation-2026-07-25`  
**Scope:** World Economic Forum links to Columbus institutions, personnel, public-private governance, and locally funded implementation capacity.

## Executive judgment

The public record supports a **direct and operationally relevant WEF–Columbus policy-network relationship**.

The strongest documented chain is:

1. the World Economic Forum maintains an official **City of Columbus** organization profile associated with its **Centre for Urban Transformation**;
2. **Jordan Davis**, identified by WEF as Executive Director of **Smart Columbus**, served on the task force for a WEF city-infrastructure policy roadmap;
3. Smart Columbus operates inside the **Columbus Partnership**, a CEO-led regional organization built around public-private and academic coordination;
4. Smart Columbus has a board joining municipal, county, regional-planning and corporate representatives;
5. the City of Columbus pays Smart Columbus to perform thought-leadership, convening, project-delivery, climate, electrification, broadband and digital-equity work.

This is sufficient to describe **documented influence through participation, access, policy development and implementation capacity**. It is not sufficient to claim that WEF commands Columbus, funds the City directly, ordered a specific policy, diverted public money, or committed fraud.

## 1. Direct institutional link

WEF maintains an official organization profile titled **City of Columbus**. The profile displays the **Centre for Urban Transformation**, which WEF describes as advancing public-private collaboration in cities and supporting resilient, future-ready communities and local economies.

This establishes a direct institutional association in WEF's own records. The profile does not disclose:

- when the relationship began;
- whether Columbus pays dues or sponsorships;
- whether a formal agreement exists;
- which City office authorized participation;
- whether Columbus accepted binding commitments;
- what deliverables or policy changes resulted.

The profile is therefore evidence of engagement, not evidence of control.

## 2. Smart Columbus personnel joined WEF policy development

The WEF Centre for Urban Transformation's project **Implementing a Life-Cycle Approach to Infrastructure: a Policy Roadmap for Cities** lists **Jordan Davis, Executive Director, Smart Columbus** as a task-force member.

The task force included representatives from infrastructure companies, consultancies, policy organizations and city governments. Its output was a policy roadmap intended to help cities apply a life-cycle approach to infrastructure planning and implementation.

This is the clearest personnel-level bridge found:

```text
WEF Centre for Urban Transformation
                |
                | task-force participation
                v
           Jordan Davis
                |
                | Executive Director
                v
          Smart Columbus
```

The record proves participation in WEF policy development. It does not prove that Columbus adopted the roadmap, that Davis represented the City under a formal mandate, or that WEF directed a Columbus project.

## 3. Local transmission and implementation layer

Smart Columbus describes itself as the region's **civic innovation lab**. Its stated operating model includes:

- aligning public, private and nonprofit partners;
- co-designing solutions;
- prototyping and piloting projects;
- refining projects for wider deployment;
- using technology to address community problems.

Smart Columbus also states that it is part of the broader **Columbus Partnership**.

The Columbus Partnership describes itself as a nonprofit, member-based organization of CEOs from leading regional businesses and institutions. It coordinates economic development, smart-city work, sustainability, government relations and other regional priorities through the Partnership, One Columbus and Smart Columbus.

This matters because WEF engagement does not terminate at a conference or publication. The participating local organization sits inside a regional structure designed to convert cross-sector priorities into projects, policy support and implementation.

## 4. Formal public-private governance interface

The official Smart Columbus board page lists representatives from:

- the **City of Columbus**;
- **Franklin County**;
- the **Mid-Ohio Regional Planning Commission**;
- the **Columbus Partnership**;
- **IBM iX**;
- nonprofit and private-sector organizations.

The One Columbus Executive Committee separately joins the Columbus mayor, a Franklin County commissioner, JobsOhio, financial institutions, utilities, healthcare firms and Battelle.

These are formal coordination bodies, not merely informal professional overlap. They provide a plausible channel through which global frameworks, corporate expertise and local government priorities can be translated into regional initiatives.

Board membership alone does not prove agreement, policy capture or external command.

## 5. City-funded implementation capacity

Six identified City ordinances between 2022 and 2026 authorized up to **$2,733,574.98** for Smart Columbus work:

| Ordinance | Authorized ceiling | Stated area |
|---|---:|---|
| 0670-2022 | $263,574.98 | continuation after the federal Smart City grant |
| 2657-2022 | $560,000.00 | broadband and digital equity |
| 1462-2023 | $500,000.00 | operational support |
| 2266-2023 | $610,000.00 | broadband and digital equity |
| 1663-2025 | $500,000.00 | operational support and City initiatives |
| 1850-2026 | $300,000.00 | operational support and City initiatives |
| **Identified total** | **$2,733,574.98** | |

The 2025 and 2026 ordinances state that Smart Columbus will lead private-sector elements of the City's Climate Action Plan and electrification efforts and assist with broadband and digital-equity initiatives. The 2026 ordinance also describes a framework for municipal thought leadership, partner convening and project delivery.

These authorizations show that Smart Columbus possesses publicly funded implementation capacity. They do **not** show that City money was transferred to WEF. Authorized ceilings are not equivalent to confirmed outlays.

## 6. WEF-linked corporate overlap

Two additional network overlaps are directly documented:

- **Accenture** appears in WEF's partner network, is listed as a One Columbus investor, and supplied a lead for the WEF infrastructure-roadmap task force.
- **IBM** appears in WEF's 2026 partner list, while an IBM iX representative sits on the Smart Columbus board.

This raises network density and access, but the evidence must remain bounded. Corporate participation across WEF and Columbus institutions does not by itself prove a coordinated agenda or control relationship.

## 7. Best-supported influence model

The evidence supports the following mechanism:

```text
WEF urban-policy network
        |
        | frameworks, task forces, peer exchange
        v
Smart Columbus personnel and organization
        |
        | Columbus Partnership governance
        v
City + county + regional planning + corporate board
        |
        | City-funded convening, strategy and delivery
        v
local programmes and implementation
```

The relationship may also operate in reverse. Columbus projects can be presented as case studies or practical models and redistributed through WEF networks. The current record supports policy diffusion and institutional access more strongly than one-way command.

## Claims not established

The reviewed sources do not establish:

- direct WEF funding to the City of Columbus;
- direct WEF funding to Smart Columbus;
- a binding WEF membership or partnership agreement;
- WEF authorship of a specific Columbus ordinance;
- WEF authority to approve or veto local policy;
- public-money diversion to WEF;
- bribery, fraud or procurement corruption;
- a unified command structure connecting every WEF-linked corporation in Columbus.

## Priority records

The highest-value next collection is documentary:

1. City, Smart Columbus and Columbus Partnership correspondence with WEF and the Centre for Urban Transformation.
2. Membership, partnership, sponsorship or participation agreements.
3. WEF-related invoices, dues, donations, travel and conference expenses.
4. Task-force invitations, agendas, minutes, drafts and final deliverables.
5. Internal records showing whether WEF roadmaps or toolkits were circulated or used.
6. Smart Columbus board minutes, work plans and project-selection records.
7. Ordinance scopes of work, invoices and deliverables for City-funded Smart Columbus contracts.
8. Records identifying who requested or approved the WEF City of Columbus profile.
9. Side-by-side comparison of WEF framework language with Columbus policy drafts and procurement documents.

## Analytical conclusion

Columbus is not merely home to companies that also participate in WEF.

The direct record shows a City-level WEF profile, a Smart Columbus executive on a WEF policy task force, and a local intermediary with formal public-private governance and City-funded implementation responsibilities. That is a defensible influence finding.

The unresolved question is not whether a connection exists. It is **how far the connection reaches into specific policy decisions, contracts and programme designs**.
