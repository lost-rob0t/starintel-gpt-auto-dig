## Adjudicated Ohio conduct

Marcus Williamson pleaded guilty on March 23, 2026 to **nine robbery counts** arising from a series of Franklin County bank robberies committed between March 7 and April 24, 2024. Five counts were second-degree felonies and four were third-degree felonies. Assistant Franklin County Prosecutor Jason Manning said that in five robberies Williamson stated or implied that he had a gun. Reporting based on court records places the total amount taken at approximately **$22,000**.

This conduct is adjudicated and may be represented as convictions/guilty pleas rather than allegations.

## Separate 2022 Tennessee violent kidnapping / aggravated-robbery incident

In August 2022, Williamson was arrested in Shelby County, Tennessee after an incident in which the complainant reported that the suspect **appeared to have or implied he had a weapon**, took her wallet and phone, forced her to drive to an ATM, forced a **$500 withdrawal**, made her exit the vehicle, and drove away in her car. Police later found Williamson driving the vehicle; the complainant identified him in a photo lineup.

Reported charges included **two counts of aggravated robbery, especially aggravated kidnapping, and evidence tampering/fabrication**.

This was a reported violent criminal incident, not a generic “episode.” It must nevertheless **not** be represented as a conviction: public reporting says Columbus police Detective Brad Thomas later testified that the Tennessee charges had been dismissed / not prosecuted because of competency concerns. The Tennessee docket still should be obtained to establish the exact legal disposition from the court itself.

## Sentencing-disparity assessment

**Assessment: SUPPORTED — descriptive sentencing disparity.**

This classification means the sentence is materially more lenient than multiple relevant Franklin County robbery comparators and than the ordinary statutory starting point for second-degree felonies. It does **not** by itself establish why the disparity occurred.

### Statutory baseline

Ohio Revised Code **R.C. 2929.13(D)(1)** creates a presumption that a prison term is necessary for first- and second-degree felonies. Under **R.C. 2929.13(D)(2)**, a court may overcome that presumption and impose community control only after finding both that non-prison sanctions adequately punish the offender/protect the public and that they do not demean the seriousness of the offense. Ohio law also directs courts to impose sentences consistent with sentences for similar crimes committed by similar offenders. Williamson pleaded guilty to **five F2 robberies**, so the prison presumption applied to multiple counts.

The closest historical comparator located so far is **State v. Gilbert**. Like Williamson, Gilbert committed a prepared series of bank/hotel robberies and claimed to have a gun. Gilbert received 51 years for 11 robbery convictions. The Tenth District specifically observed that the repeated, carefully prepared robberies and their sheer number supported a significant term of incarceration. Williamson's reported zero-immediate-prison sentence for nine bank robberies is therefore not merely “lenient” in the abstract; it is a **large observable sentencing difference inside the same county and appellate district**.

The Jarrett and Cunningham cases have aggravating facts that make them imperfect comparators, so they cannot be used to claim that Williamson should have received their exact sentences. They nevertheless reinforce that substantial immediate incarceration is a documented outcome for serial robbery conduct in Franklin County.

## What the disparity does and does not establish

### Supported

- Williamson received an unusually non-custodial immediate disposition relative to the located Franklin County robbery comparators.
- The disposition overcame Ohio's statutory presumption favoring prison on each F2 robbery count.
- A serious comparative-sentencing question is warranted and should remain part of the dataset.

### Not established

- That Williamson's former Ohio State football status caused the sentence.
- That political favoritism caused the sentence.
- That Judge Munson's political affiliation caused the sentence.
- That any comparator is perfectly identical after controlling for criminal history, plea concessions, presentence investigation findings, competency evidence, treatment eligibility, restitution, victim-impact evidence, and all R.C. 2929.12 factors.

A causal favoritism claim requires a larger matched cohort and the actual sentencing transcript/judgment. The current evidence is sufficient for **sentencing disparity**, but not for a supported explanation of its cause.

## Judge metadata

Public election records identify **Sheryl K. Munson as a Democratic judicial candidate**. A 2020 judicial-candidate profile also listed affiliations with the Franklin County Democratic Party and Franklin County Democratic Lawyers Club. That supports describing her political affiliation as Democratic; it does **not** establish an affiliation with the Democratic National Committee (DNC), and party affiliation is not evidence that politics caused this sentence.

Munson also directs Franklin County's **RISE Mental Health Court**, a specialty docket intended to provide treatment-oriented alternatives for qualifying felony offenders whose serious mental illness was a primary factor in justice-system involvement. That institutional role is relevant context for understanding her stated sentencing philosophy, but it does not erase the violent nature of the underlying conduct or prove that a particular sentence was legally correct or incorrect.
